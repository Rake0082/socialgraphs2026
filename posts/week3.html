Who bridges the tangled web?

Degree tells you how popular a character is. Betweenness tells you something different: how often you'd have to walk through them to get from one part of the network to another. To find out which is which, we shuffled the network 200 times — keeping every character's friend-count exactly fixed — and recomputed betweenness each time. The z-score below is how many standard deviations a character's real betweenness sits above that degree-only expectation.

more of a bridge than degree alone predicts less of a bridge than degree alone predicts

Cloak and Dagger (degree 7) is the single biggest surprise in the network (z = 9.3): their seven friends are Doctor Strange, Iron Fist, Venom, Emma Frost, Hulkling, Darkhawk and Robbie Baldwin — mystic heroes, street-level heroes, a mutant and two legacy teenagers who otherwise don't run in the same circles. Scarlet Witch (z = 5.2) and Doctor Strange himself (z = 4.8) play the same role for the X-Men/Avengers and the magic corner of the network, respectively. None of them are the highest-degree character — that's Wolverine, who is popular and a genuine bridge (z = 1.75, unremarkable once you account for how many friends he has).

The X-Men don't need a broker

The flip side of a bridge is a clique — a knot of characters who already all know each other, so no single one of them is ever necessary to connect the rest. The network's clique number is 5, and all three maximum cliques are built from the exact same four-person core:

Cyclops, Jean Grey, Emma Frost and Rachel Summers appear in every single one — classic X-Men ensemble-book territory, where every teammate gets drawn talking to every other teammate. That's exactly why they show up at the bottom of the bridge ranking above (Jean Grey z = −3.5, Rachel Summers z = −2.7, Cyclops z = −2.2): high degree, but every shortcut through them is already a shortcut around them too, so removing any one of them barely changes anyone's shortest path.

Hubs vs. bulletproof

Remove the giant component's 185 characters one at a time, ranked by (a) betweenness, (b) plain degree, or (c) a random shuffle, and watch how much of the network stays reachable.

remove by betweenness remove by degree remove at random (avg of 20 runs)

Either targeted strategy cuts the giant component in half after removing about 15% of the characters (28 by degree, 30 by betweenness). Random failure barely dents it — even after the same 30 removals, 77% of the network is still standing. Degree and betweenness don't agree on who to remove first, though: the degree ranking wastes early hits on Cyclops, Jean Grey and Rachel Summers — the very clique members from above, who are popular but not structurally load-bearing.

Popular knows popular

For each friend-count k, we averaged the friend-count of everyone who has k friends' neighbours — the knn(k) curve from this week's reading. A rising line means well-connected characters cluster with other well-connected characters.

knn(k), real network flat line a random mixing pattern would sit at

The line trends up (noisily — a lot of the high-k points are averages of just one or two characters), and the degree assortativity coefficient comes out to r = +0.24. Marvel's ensemble books behave like a real social network here, not like the internet: famous characters keep getting drawn standing next to other famous characters.

One name, two capes

Nine display names — Wolverine, Jean Grey, Captain Marvel, She-Hulk, Human Torch, Spider-Woman (×3), Ghost Rider (×3), White Tiger and Union Jack — each turned up as two or three separate nodes in the graph. Marvel has reused most of these codenames for a different character at some point (a legacy Ghost Rider, an alternate-universe Jean Grey), and our Week 1 scrape resolved each Wikipedia redirect on its own, without merging pages that happen to render under the same name.

It doesn't change any of the headline numbers above — these duplicate pairs mostly form their own small, disconnected two- or three-node islands, which is also most of why the network has 8 connected components instead of 1. Worth a note for anyone trying to reproduce exact centrality ranks, though.

So who actually matters?

  • Degree ≠ betweenness. The most popular character (Wolverine) is a fine bridge; the biggest bridges (Cloak and Dagger, Scarlet Witch, Doctor Strange) are nowhere near the most popular.
  • Cliques cancel betweenness. The X-Men core clique has high degree and some of the lowest z-scores in the network — redundancy, not importance.
  • Targeted beats random, hands down. 15% of characters removed the smart way does what removing them at random can't, even after 30 tries.
  • The network is (mildly) assortative, r = +0.24 — unlike most infrastructure networks, but typical for a social one.
Verdict: not all friends are created equal
