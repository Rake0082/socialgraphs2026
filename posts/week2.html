Six Degrees of Spider-Man

Any two of the 277 connected characters are 2.7 hops apart. Shuffle every link keeping each character's friend-count fixed and you get… 2.6. Marvel's short paths aren't a feature — they're what randomness hands you for free.

So where do real networks differ? Watts & Strogatz: start from a tidy ring where everyone knows their neighbours (lots of triangles, long paths), then rewire a fraction q of links to random targets.

clustering C, as a share of the ring's mean distance, as a share of the ring's small-world window

A handful of shortcuts (q ≈ 1–5%) collapse the distance while the triangles barely notice. That gap — short paths and high clustering — is the small-world signature. Marvel sits deep inside it.

The Shuffle Test

Pick a number. Shuffle the network 100 times — keeping every character's friend-count exactly — and see where the real value lands. Far out in the tail = real structure. Sitting in the pile = nothing but the degree sequence talking.

degree-preserving shuffle random G(n,m) the real Marvel network

z-score = how many standard deviations the real value sits from the null mean. Empirical p = 1/101 means not one of the 100 shuffles was as extreme.

Why Your Friends Are Cooler Than You

Grab a random character: 10 friends on average. Now grab a random one of their friends: 22. Same network, double the friends — because the popular characters (Spidey, Hulk, Wolverine) show up in everybody's friend list.

Only the biggest hubs beat their friends — Spider-Man (106 friends, friends average 15) and about 34 others. For everyone else, the paradox bites. It survives a degree-preserving shuffle too: it's baked into having a lopsided friend-count distribution at all.

So is the Marvel network special?

We ran every quirk past its random twin. The scorecard:

  • Short paths — ordinary. A hair longer than the shuffle, even.
  • Triangles & clustering — real. 3× above a degree-matched shuffle (z ≈ 20), 8× above pure random.
  • The Spider-Man hub — real. No random model with Marvel's density grows a degree-106 node.
  • Reciprocity — real, but smaller than it first looks: pick the right null and "13× chance" shrinks to "8×".
  • The Lonely 17 (from Issue #1) — not special. A degree-preserving null reproduces all 17 exactly. They're just "17 nodes have zero links", nothing more.
  • A power law? — unproven. Looks the part over one decade, but 303 characters is too few to fit one honestly.
Verdict: real structure, mostly
